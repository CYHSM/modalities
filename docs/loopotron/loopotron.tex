\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A Nemotron-style hybrid language model is a stack of single-operator residual layers --- Mamba-2
mixers, sparse mixture-of-experts feed-forwards, and grouped-query attention --- described by a
pattern string. Because each layer is a self-contained residual block, a \emph{loop} (executing a
run of layers several times while reusing one set of weights) can be placed around any subset of
layer types. This trades parameters for effective depth, and lets us ask where extra depth is worth
spending. Wave 1 trains ten models at a matched budget of 557M tokens: an unlooped hybrid baseline,
three looped variants, three conditioning refinements, an iso-FLOP fresh-weight anchor, and two
vanilla dense transformers. Three results survive a measured seed-noise floor. First, \textbf{every
looped variant beats the unlooped hybrid} on held-out loss, by 12--48 standard deviations of
run-to-run noise. Second, \textbf{the fresh-weight anchor still beats every looped variant} at
matched FLOPs, reproducing the published perplexity penalty for weight sharing. Third, and
largest, \textbf{the hybrid beats an iso-FLOP dense transformer by 0.198 nats}. We also report two
negative results: per-iteration normalization and input injection have no measurable effect, and a
synthetic reasoning suite designed to detect the reasoning-over-perplexity trade-off has
insufficient resolution at this budget --- a claim we initially made from it, and here retract.

Wave 2 (Section~\ref{sec:wave2}) re-runs the ablation at $\sim$9$\times$ the token budget (4.997B
tokens, roughly the compute-optimal allocation for this model's active parameters) and completes
the three single-operator loops --- Mamba alone, MoE alone, attention alone --- that Wave 1 left
untested, together with their fresh-weight anchors. Wave 2 also changes tokenizer, dataset and
hardware layout alongside the budget, so absolute nats do not compare across waves; only rankings
do. Two results are robust across both waves: \textbf{every looped variant still beats the unlooped
baseline}, and \textbf{a fresh-weight anchor still beats its iso-execution looped counterpart} ---
all eleven Wave 2 arms finished, confirming the latter for all four matched anchor/loop pairs,
including a direct re-confirmation of Wave 1's original attention+MoE pair. The newly completed
single-operator arms answer the titular question directly: \textbf{looping Mamba alone is the best
loop of any kind tried, looping attention alone is the worst} --- the depth this architecture wants
is concentrated in the recurrent operator, not the quadratic one, and that same operator shows the
largest fresh-vs-shared-weight gap of the four pairs. Twelve additional 5B-token runs (two extra
seeds of each A1--A6) were launched to check these rankings against a measured seed-noise floor
rather than $n=1$ (Section~\ref{sec:wave2-seeds}). All six are now replicated at three seeds, and
\textbf{the ranking is unchanged}, with A1's lead and A3's deficit several times the measured noise
while the interior ordering is not resolved. Five of the twelve extra runs had to be discarded and
re-run: they were contaminated by a scheduling artifact --- a run resumed with \texttt{modalities
warmstart} is not interchangeable with an uninterrupted one, which we traced to a checkpointing
defect on the tied embedding/output weight rather than to the token budget or the learning-rate
schedule, both verified identical. It shifted their apparent quality by 0.04--0.055 nats, an order
of magnitude past genuine seed noise, and would have overturned the paper's own headline finding had
it gone unnoticed. Those numbers are excluded, not averaged in; their clean replacements land within
0.002 nats of the arms' original seeds.

Wave 3 (Section~\ref{sec:wave3}) revisits the negative result on loop refinements by making loop
count the independent variable, running the best loop at $K{=}3$, $K{=}6$ and $K{=}12$ against three
refinements from the recent literature: a stabilized recurrence with eigenvalues bounded in $(0,1)$
by construction, an injection norm, and FiLM iteration conditioning. Four results. First,
\textbf{loop depth has an optimum near $K{=}6$}: the plain loop improves from $K{=}3$ to $K{=}6$
and then gets \emph{worse} at $K{=}12$, which is worse than $K{=}3$ despite three times the executed
depth. Second, \textbf{the predicted instability is real and appears exactly where the literature
says}: gradient norms never exceed 7 at $K{=}3$ or $K{=}6$ and peak at 85{,}782 at $K{=}12$. Third,
\textbf{the stabilizer does not stabilize it} --- given a genuine instability to fix it leaves the
loss unchanged (0.2 s.d.) and produces more large-gradient steps than the plain loop --- while
\textbf{FiLM iteration conditioning, which carries no stability guarantee, cuts the peak gradient
norm 37-fold} and is the only refinement to improve the loss at every depth. Fourth, the injection
norm is \textbf{actively harmful}, costing 0.0475 nats at $K{=}12$ and dominating any combination it
appears in, so that a single bundled ``all refinements'' arm would have concealed which component
did the damage. Wave 3's $K{=}3$ control is architecturally identical to Wave 2's best arm and serves
as a fourth seed of it, raising that arm's measured seed s.d.\ from 0.0013 to 0.0021 --- a reminder,
applied throughout, that a standard deviation from three runs is itself uncertain by about half its
value.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Nemotron-style hybrid models~\cite{nemotron} interleave three operator types according to a layer
pattern string, in which \lp{M} denotes a Mamba-2 mixer, \lp{E} a sparse mixture-of-experts
feed-forward, \lp{*} grouped-query attention, and \lp{-} a dense feed-forward. Unlike a classical
transformer block, which bundles attention and a feed-forward network, every layer here wraps
exactly one operator in a pre-norm residual connection,
\begin{equation}
x \leftarrow x + f(\mathrm{norm}(x)),
\end{equation}
so the ratio of Mamba to attention to MoE layers is tunable independently.

This structure makes a natural experiment available. Because each layer is self-contained, a
\emph{loop} --- executing a run of layers several times while reusing one set of weights, as in a
Universal Transformer --- can be placed around any subset of layer types. Looping holds parameters
fixed while raising FLOPs, so it converts parameters into effective depth. The question this work
asks is: \textbf{where is that depth worth spending --- on the Mamba mixers, the MoE
feed-forwards, the attention layers, or on mixed blocks?}

The published consensus is that depth-wise weight sharing loses on perplexity and wins on
reasoning~\cite{saunshi,lssm}. That expectation shaped the design: we measure held-out loss and a
synthetic reasoning suite together, and we include a fresh-weight \emph{anchor} for the leading
looped variant so that ``more compute helps'' can be separated from ``sharing works''.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Loop grammar}

A loop group is written \lp{[\textless symbols\textgreater]\caret K} inside a layer pattern; for
example \lp{M[ME]\caret3*E} builds one Mamba and one MoE layer and executes the pair three times.
The repeat operator is \lp{\caret} rather than \lp{*}, because \lp{*} is the attention symbol and
\lp{M[ME]*3*E} would read as though the 3 were multiplied by an attention layer. Nesting is
unsupported. Bracket-free patterns parse exactly as before, so all pre-existing configurations are
unaffected. Throughout, \emph{built} layers count sets of weights and \emph{executed} layers count
applications per token, so \lp{M[ME]\caret3E} has 4 built and 8 executed layers.

The implementation keeps the layer container flat, with one entry per set of weights, and puts the
loop in a separate execution schedule. This is what allows activation checkpointing, FSDP2 block
wrapping, the weight-decay group regular expressions and the initialization parameter filters to
work on looped models unchanged, since parameter names are untouched.

Three quantities must follow \emph{executions} rather than parameters, and getting any of them
wrong biases an ablation silently rather than raising an error:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Effective depth} for depth-scaled initialization. Residual variance grows with the
    number of layer applications, so a looped variant must be initialized for its executed depth.
    This is the single most important detail; the configuration derives it from the pattern so it
    cannot go stale.
  \item \textbf{The MoE auxiliary load-balancing loss}, which is accumulated across visits and
    reset per forward pass. A looped MoE layer routes independently on each visit.
  \item \textbf{Active parameters} for the FLOPs estimate, which are execution-weighted.
\end{itemize}

The model is 1.1B rather than 400M by necessity: the embedding table and the Mamba layers are dense
and always active, so a 400M model with a 128k vocabulary cannot be $\sim$10\% active, and forcing
the size down distorts the $M : * : E$ cost ratio, which is exactly what the ablation is about.
Training FLOPs scale with \emph{active} parameters, so these models train at roughly the speed of a
225M dense model; only memory holds 1.1B.

\subsection{Training setup}

Every run is identical except where stated: FineWeb (2B-token shard), sequence length 2048, global
batch $8 \times 4 \times 2048 = 65{,}536$ tokens per step, AdamW ($\beta = (0.9, 0.95)$, weight
decay 0.1 excluding embeddings, norms, SSM parameters and the router), linear warmup over 750 steps
followed by cosine annealing from $4.5\times10^{-4}$ to $4.5\times10^{-5}$, bf16 mixed precision,
full activation checkpointing, one A100-80GB per run. All models train for
\textbf{8500 steps = 557M tokens}, and the learning-rate schedule completes within that budget.

That last point is not a detail. An earlier wave derived the schedule length from the size of the
training file (30{,}517 steps) while stopping runs on wall clock at 5--11k steps, so the cosine
never decayed: the learning rate was still at 96\% of its peak at step 4750 and the loss flattened
at 3.63 against the 3.34 reached once the schedule completed. Worse for an ablation than for the
absolute number, a constant peak learning rate puts every variant on the same noisy plateau. All
results below use the completed schedule.

\subsection{Evaluations}

Because the published effect is expected to live in reasoning rather than perplexity, we log four
held-out measures alongside the language-modelling loss. Two are real benchmarks scored by the
negative log-likelihood of the answer span: \textbf{Minerva MATH} (4-shot) and closed-book
\textbf{TriviaQA}, the latter acting as a parametric-knowledge control, since closed-book recall
lives in weights that a loop does not add. Accuracy on both is meaningless at this scale and is not
reported.

Two are synthetic and deterministic, parameterized by a \emph{hop count} --- the number of serial
lookups the answer requires --- over a 26-symbol alphabet, giving a chance accuracy of
$1/26 = 3.85\%$. In \textbf{$p$-hop induction}, a sequence of 256 random symbols is presented; the
last symbol is the query, and one hop finds its most recent earlier occurrence and reads off the
symbol that follows it. Each further hop repeats this, searching only to the left of the previous
match, so hop $k+1$ cannot begin until hop $k$ has produced both its symbol and its position. One
hop is exactly the induction-head operation and acts as the control. For example, over a 6-symbol
alphabet:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l@{}}
\ttfamily E~A~B~B~B~E~F~D~A~A~B~C~D~C~B~A~E~E~A~A \\[-0.2em]
\ttfamily \phantom{E~A~B~B~B~E~F~D~A~A~B~C~D~C~B~}$\uparrow$\phantom{A~E~}$\uparrow$~$\uparrow$ \\[-0.2em]
\ttfamily \phantom{E~A~B~B~B~E~F~D~A~A~B~C~D~C~B~}\,2\phantom{A~E~}\,1~q \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Reading right to left: \texttt{q} marks the query \texttt{A}; hop 1 finds its most recent earlier
occurrence and takes the symbol following it, again \texttt{A}; hop 2 searches only further left,
finds \texttt{A} at position 15 and takes \texttt{E}, the 2-hop answer. In \textbf{variable binding}, a
\emph{shuffled} list of assignments dereferences a chain to one literal, so recency heuristics do
not help; a real sample is
\begin{center}
\ttfamily ; F = S ; E = H ; L = A ; T = U ; P = G ; Z = V ; N = D ; H = N ; O = K ; W = Y ; J = C ; Q = R ; E =
\end{center}
whose answer is \texttt{D} via $E \to H \to N \to D$. This task has a format-aware shortcut: the
answer is always a symbol appearing as a value but never as a variable, and there are exactly ten
such, so a guesser exploiting the format reaches 10\% without following the chain. It produced no
signal in any run and is not reported below.

Every sample is emitted pre-shifted and masked so that only the answer position is scored; the
reported figure is therefore the negative log-likelihood, or accuracy, of the answer alone, with
each scored token weighted equally rather than each batch.

\subsection{Variants}

Table~\ref{tab:main} lists the models. The looped variants are matched on \textbf{FLOPs, not loop
count}: an extra MoE execution costs about twice an extra Mamba execution and attention about
1.65$\times$, so equal $K$ across types would compare unequal compute budgets. The loop is also
spread over \emph{every} layer of the relevant type rather than concentrated on one, so ``which
layer happened to be looped'' is not a confound.

Two comparisons matter. Against the unlooped baseline \textbf{A0} (iso-parameter) the question is
whether looping buys usable compute. Against the \emph{anchor} \textbf{N4} --- a bracket-free model
with the same executed count of every layer type, the same active parameters and the same FLOPs as
A6, differing only in that its executions use fresh weights --- the question is whether sharing is
as good as not sharing. Two vanilla dense transformers (pre-norm, RoPE, SwiGLU, multi-head
attention, tied embeddings) supply the reference the hybrid literature usually omits: \textbf{D1}
matches A0's \emph{active} parameters and therefore its FLOPs per token, while \textbf{D2} matches
A0's \emph{total} parameters and consequently spends about 4.9$\times$ the compute per token.

Finally, three conditioning refinements are applied to A6. \textbf{Per-iteration normalization}
gives each loop iteration its own pre-norm while the operator stays shared, adding $(K-1) \cdot
d_{\text{model}}$ parameters per looped layer (4096 in total here). \textbf{Input injection} adds
the loop group's input back to the hidden states at the start of every iteration after the first,
adding no parameters. Both are described in the literature as near-prerequisites for scaling past
two or three loop iterations, and both are deliberately optional so that weight sharing and
per-iteration conditioning remain separable.

The consequences point in opposite directions.

\paragraph{Held-out loss resolves the ordering.} At a standard deviation of $0.0005$, every
difference in Table~\ref{tab:main} is far outside noise. N4 versus A0 (0.038) is roughly 76 standard
deviations; even the smallest gap, A6b versus A0 (0.006), is roughly 12. The ordering
anchor $>$ looped variants $>$ unlooped baseline $>$ iso-FLOP dense is therefore real.

\paragraph{The reasoning suite does not resolve anything.} The seed standard deviation on
$p$-hop-1 accuracy (0.0172) exceeds the entire spread across hybrid variants, and the standard
deviations on MATH ($\pm0.0177$) and TriviaQA ($\pm0.0629$) cover every difference between hybrid
rows in Table~\ref{tab:main}. Resolving a 0.02 difference in $p$-hop accuracy at two standard
deviations would require roughly twelve runs per variant. Crucially, more evaluation questions
would not help: the sampling standard error over 2048 questions is 0.007, so the dominant variance
is in the model, not the question sample.

\subsection{Wave 1: findings}

\paragraph{1. Looping helps, modestly and reliably.} Every looped variant beats the unlooped
baseline A0 by 0.006--0.024 nats, or 12--48 standard deviations. The best is A5, which loops
Mamba-plus-attention pairs three times.

\paragraph{2. Fresh weights still beat shared weights at matched FLOPs.} N4 (3.3021) beats A6
(3.3243), its exact iso-FLOP anchor, by 0.022 nats. This reproduces the published perplexity
penalty for depth-wise weight sharing. What the loop buys instead is memory: A6 reaches the same
executed depth with 1.105B parameters against N4's 1.487B.

\paragraph{3. The hybrid beats an iso-FLOP dense transformer by 0.198 nats}, comparing A0 to D1.
This is by far the largest effect measured, and it is the comparison most often missing from
hybrid-architecture ablations. It comes with a caveat: D1 trains at 34.5 samples/s against A0's
23.3 (MFU 0.36 versus 0.22), because dense attention and a plain SwiGLU use the hardware far better
than Mamba and MoE kernels. Per unit of \emph{wall clock} rather than of FLOPs, the hybrid's
advantage is substantially smaller.

\paragraph{4. Per-iteration normalization and input injection do nothing measurable.} A6a
(3.3246), A6c (3.3301) and A6b (3.3339) straddle A6 (3.3243). We initially reported that
per-iteration norms improved $p$-hop-1 accuracy from 0.073 to 0.108, a nominal 3.9 standard
deviations. That figure used the \emph{sampling} standard error over the evaluation set (0.007) in
place of run-to-run noise (0.017), which it understates by a factor of 2.5. Under replication,
0.108 proved to be the top of A6a's own seed range and A6's 0.073 essentially the bottom of it; the
difference is 1.2 standard deviations. \textbf{We retract the claim.} Sampling error is a lower
bound on measurement error, never a substitute for it.

\paragraph{5. The expected reasoning-over-perplexity dissociation did not appear.} MATH and
TriviaQA were included expecting them to move in opposite directions if a variant bought reasoning
without buying parameters. They do not: both track held-out loss almost perfectly across all ten
models, with N4 among the best and D1 the worst on all three. No variant is trading knowledge for
reasoning at this budget.

\paragraph{6. Block-loop underperforms layer- and pair-loop.} A4, which repeats a Mamba-plus-MoE
block, trails on every reasoning measure, consistent with reports that repeating each layer in
place beats repeating the stack.

\paragraph{7. A dense-model anomaly, resolved by scale.} D1 scores 0.0073 average $p$-hop accuracy
--- reliably \emph{below} the 0.0385 chance level, which requires structure rather than ignorance
--- while scoring normally on variable binding, which uses the same alphabet and the same
evaluation path. D2, the same architecture with 4.9$\times$ the compute, does not collapse
(0.0527). The effect is therefore one of scale and budget, not of architecture class: a small dense
transformer at 557M tokens has not formed the in-context machinery to notice that the answer must
be one of the 26 symbols present, whereas Mamba's recurrent state tracks the in-context
distribution essentially for free, and every hybrid lands near $\ln 26 = 3.26$ nats.

\subsection{Wave 2: the 5B-token wave}
\label{sec:wave2}

Wave 1 left two things unresolved: the three single-operator loops --- Mamba alone, MoE alone,
attention alone --- were never run, and 557M tokens is a small fraction of a compute-optimal budget.
Wave 2 addresses both: it adds \textbf{A1} (\lp{[M]\caret3} at $K{=}3$), \textbf{A2}
(\lp{[E]\caret2} at $K{=}2$) and \textbf{A3} (\lp{[*]\caret4} at $K{=}4$) together with their
fresh-weight anchors \textbf{N1--N3}, re-runs \textbf{A0} and \textbf{A4--A6} with their anchor
\textbf{N4}, and trains all eleven arms to \textbf{76{,}250 steps = 4{,}997{,}120{,}000 tokens}
($\approx$5B, $\sim$9$\times$ Wave 1) on the Leonardo cluster.

\paragraph{What changed besides the budget.} Four things moved together with the token count, so
Wave 2 is not a clean scaling ladder on Wave 1 and \textbf{absolute nats are not comparable across
waves} --- only rankings within a wave are.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tokenizer.} Llama-3-8B (vocab 128{,}256) $\to$ GPT-2 (vocab 50{,}304), which alone
    shifts every embedding-table parameter count and, since the two tokenizers segment text
    differently, the nats-per-token scale itself.
  \item \textbf{Data.} Wave 1's unfiltered 2B-token FineWeb shard $\to$ FineWeb-edu, stratified into
    quality-score buckets (3.0--3.5, 3.5--4.0, 4.0--4.5, 4.5+); train draws 510 shards spanning all
    four buckets, and test draws 2 shards from the 4.5+ bucket only --- deliberately the smallest,
    after an earlier attempt to stratify test across buckets produced a $\sim$585k-sample eval set
    that stalled a single evaluation call for over 8 minutes, impractical across 305 scheduled
    evaluations.
  \item \textbf{Hardware.} One A100 per run $\to$ one Leonardo node (4$\times$A100) per run, FSDP2
    pure-replicate at $\text{dp}=4$. The global batch is unchanged at $8 \times 4 \times 2048 =
    65{,}536$ tokens/step; the 4$\times$ that was gradient accumulation on a single GPU in Wave 1 is
    data-parallel replication across 4 GPUs in Wave 2. The node count was chosen empirically on the
    debug queue: $\text{dp}=4$ (1 node) measured 58--84 samples/s depending on arm, a clean
    $\sim$4$\times$ over a single GPU, while $\text{dp}=16$ (4 nodes) measured \emph{worse}
    ($\sim$38 samples/s) because cross-node all-reduce overhead and thinner per-GPU MoE routing
    batches outweigh the extra GPUs at this model size. Every arm uses the same node count, since
    arms are compared on their answers to the same seeded data stream and that stream's order
    depends on world size.
  \item \textbf{Warmup fraction.} 750 of 8500 steps (8.8\%) $\to$ 1500 of 76{,}250 (2.0\%); both are
    linear warmup into the same cosine decay from $4.5\times10^{-4}$ to $4.5\times10^{-5}$, chosen
    for absolute step count rather than held fixed as a fraction of the (much longer) Wave 2
    schedule.
\end{itemize}
On the budget itself: 4.997B tokens is close to the Chinchilla-style compute-optimal allocation for
this model's 225--278M \emph{active} parameters ($\sim$20 tokens/parameter $\Rightarrow$
$\sim$4.5--5.6B), though by the same heuristic its 1.03--1.96B \emph{total} parameters would still
want $\sim$20--40B tokens, so Wave 2 is optimal by the active-parameter convention and undertrained
by the total-parameter one.

\subsection{Robustness across budgets}

Comparing waves means comparing the four architectures they share: A0, A4, A5, A6 and their anchor
N4. All four have now finished (N4 completed after the first draft of this section), so the
anchor-vs-loop comparison below uses the original N4/A6 pair directly, alongside the three new
single-operator pairs Wave 1 did not have.

\paragraph{Loop beats baseline, at both budgets.} Wave 1: A5 (3.3168) $<$ A4 (3.3222) $<$ A6
(3.3243) $<$ A0 (3.3404). Wave 2, by seed mean: A4 (2.5041) $<$ A5 (2.5185) $<$ A6 (2.5216) $<$ A0
(2.5385). The qualitative result --- every one of the three shared looped variants beats the
unlooped baseline --- reproduces exactly. What does \emph{not} reproduce is which loop is best: A4
and A5 swap first and second place between waves, by 0.0054 nats at 557M and 0.0144 nats at 5B.

With three seeds of each arm now in hand, the Wave 2 half of that swap is no longer an $n=1$
observation. A4 (2.5041 $\pm$ 0.0050) beats A5 (2.5185 $\pm$ 0.0010) by 0.0144 nats, which a
Welch test puts at $t = 4.9$ on 2.2 degrees of freedom --- just past the 5\% level, so the ordering
at 5B is supported but not comfortably so. The Wave 1 half remains $n=1$ per arm, and its 0.0054 nat
margin is 11$\times$ that wave's own measured seed s.d.\ (0.0005, Table~\ref{tab:noise}). Taking the
two together, the swap is a real effect of something that changed between waves rather than seed
noise --- but the four things that changed (token budget, tokenizer, data, hardware layout;
Section~\ref{sec:wave2}) moved together, so \emph{which} of them causes it is not identified by this
experiment and would need a controlled ladder to settle. The coarser ordering is what is robust: A4
and A5 both beat A6 in both waves (by 0.002--0.017 nats) and all three beat A0 in both waves (by
0.018--0.034 nats), so A6 third and A0 worst holds at both budgets even though first place does not.

\paragraph{Fresh weights still beat shared weights, now confirmed for every operator.} Wave 1
established this for the attention+MoE pair: N4 (3.3021) beat its iso-execution loop A6 (3.3243) by
0.0222 nats. All eleven Wave 2 arms have since finished, and every one of the four matched
anchor/loop pairs reproduces it independently:

(largest loop-vs-baseline gain) and therefore also has the most left on the table when those weights
are made independent instead (largest fresh-vs-shared gap). The two robustness findings are not
independent: whatever makes Mamba the operator most worth looping is the same thing that makes it
the operator most worth giving real depth instead.

\paragraph{Where the depth is worth spending: Mamba, not attention.} This is the question Wave 1
could not answer because the single-operator arms did not exist yet. Among Wave 2's six looped
variants, \textbf{A1 (loop Mamba alone) is the best of all six}, ahead of every mixed-block loop,
and \textbf{A3 (loop attention alone) is the worst}, barely ahead of the unlooped baseline (2.5312
vs.\ 2.5385, 0.0073 nats --- the smallest loop-vs-baseline margin of the six, against A1's 0.0434).
A2 (loop MoE alone, 2.5111) lands in between, statistically indistinguishable from A4's mixed
Mamba+MoE loop (2.5098; the 0.0013 gap is at A2's own seed s.d.). The ranking of looped
variants by LM loss --- A1 $<$ A4 $<$ A2 $<$ A5 $<$ A6 $<$ A3 --- puts every loop containing Mamba
ahead of every loop that does not, and the pure-attention loop dead last. Mamba's recurrent state is
also the layer type Finding~7 (Wave 1) already implicated in the hybrid's synthetic-reasoning
advantage over dense transformers; the two observations point the same direction, that this
architecture's marginal depth is worth more on the recurrent operator than on either the sparse or
the quadratic one.

\subsection{Wave 2 seed replicates}
\label{sec:wave2-seeds}

Every number above is $n=1$, and Wave 1's replicated arm (A6a, 4 seeds) was not re-run at 5B, so
Wave 2 had no seed-noise floor of its own. Two extra seeds of each A1--A6 (12 additional 5B-token
runs, same architecture and data order, independently initialized -- Section~\ref{sec:wave2}'s
seeding mechanism) were launched to close that gap and to check whether the
A1 $<$ A4 $<$ A2 $<$ A5 $<$ A6 $<$ A3 ranking and the Wave-1-vs-Wave-2 A4/A5 swap survive it.

\paragraph{An artifact, caught before it reached the table.} Five of the twelve runs were cancelled
partway through and resubmitted under a different QOS (a scheduling change unrelated to the science;
see \texttt{run\_wave\_5b\_seeds.sh}), which resumed them with \texttt{modalities warmstart} from
their last 5000-step checkpoint. All five landed 0.040--0.055 nats \emph{below} their seed1 run, an
order of magnitude past the 0.0011--0.0030 nat spread the seven genuinely-uninterrupted replicates
show (Table~\ref{tab:wave2seeds}) and past every effect size this study is built on. A sixth
cancelled run (A6\_seed3) is the control that isolates the cause: it had no checkpoint yet when
cancelled, so its resubmission started completely fresh rather than warmstarting, and it lands at
$+0.0043$ --- squarely in the uncontaminated range. The trigger is therefore the warmstart resume
itself, not the cancellation.

Two obvious explanations were checked and \emph{ruled out}: the resumed runs consumed exactly the
same 4{,}997{,}120{,}000 tokens as every other arm (so they got no extra gradient steps), and their
logged learning rate matches the single 76{,}250-step cosine schedule to six significant figures at
every checkpoint compared (so the schedule did not reset or re-warm). What a controlled reproduction
on the debug queue \emph{did} find is a defect in the checkpoint round-trip for the tied
embedding/output weight: the model is correctly tied by Python identity at every construction stage
in both code paths and after a warmstart load, but a checkpoint written by \texttt{modalities run}
contains a \texttt{transformer.lm\_head.weight} that is not the trained shared tensor at all ---
after ten training steps it is essentially orthogonal to it (cosine 0.012) and carries no optimizer
state. Loading such a checkpoint writes both parameter names into the single tied tensor, so what
the resumed model starts from depends on which name is applied last. This is enough to establish
that a warmstart-resumed run is not interchangeable with an uninterrupted one, which is all the
exclusion below rests on; it is not yet a complete account of the 0.04--0.055 nat shift, and we do
not claim one.

Averaging the affected numbers in as ordinary seed noise would have pulled A4's mean below A1's and
overturned this paper's headline finding, for a reason having nothing to do with architecture. They
are excluded here, and clean single-shot replacements (new experiment IDs, so they cannot collide
with or warmstart from the affected checkpoints) were launched under
\texttt{run\_wave\_5b\_seeds\_redo.sh} and left to run uninterrupted. All five completed the full
76{,}250 steps, and they land where uninterrupted replicates land rather than where the runs they
replaced did: A5's two sit $+0.0017$ nats from its seed1 and A6's $+0.0020$, against the $-0.042$
to $-0.044$ the discarded runs had shown. That is the cleanest available confirmation that the
excluded runs were a process artifact and not an unlucky draw of seeds.

\paragraph{Where this leaves the two headline rankings.} The ranking by mean --- A1 $<$ A4 $<$ A2
$<$ A5 $<$ A6 $<$ A3 --- is identical to the single-seed ranking this table replaces. The measured
itself worth stating: three seeds per arm reordered nothing. What replication adds is a scale against
which to read the gaps, and on that scale the ranking's ends are solid and its middle is soft.
Measured against the pooled s.d.\ of each adjacent pair, the gaps are A1$\to$A4 2.5$\times$,
A4$\to$A2 1.9$\times$, A2$\to$A5 5.8$\times$, A5$\to$A6 1.8$\times$, A6$\to$A3 5.6$\times$. So
``Mamba loop best, attention loop worst'' --- the paper's titular claim --- survives replication:
A1 leads the field and A3 trails it by margins several times the noise. The interior ordering should
not be read as a ranking at all; A5 and A6 in particular sit within two pooled standard deviations
and are best described as tied.

The Wave-1-vs-Wave-2 A4/A5 swap, which this replication wave existed to settle, is now settled on
the Wave 2 side: at $n=3$ each, A4 beats A5 by 0.0144 nats, $t = 4.9$ on 2.2 d.f.\ by a Welch test
(Section~\ref{sec:wave2}) --- past the 5\% level, though not by much. Wave 1 put the two the other
way round, at $n=1$ per arm. The swap is therefore a real effect rather than seed noise, but it
remains attributable only to ``something that changed between the waves,'' and four things changed
together (Section~\ref{sec:wave2}). Isolating which would need a controlled ladder that varies one
of them at a time, which this study does not have.

\subsection{Wave 3: loop refinements and loop depth}
\label{sec:wave3}

Wave 1 tested the two loop refinements available at the time --- per-iteration normalization and
input injection --- at $K{=}2\ldots3$ and found nothing (Finding~4). The looped-transformer
literature ablates its refinements at loop counts of eight and above, where the failure mode they
address appears: unbounded growth of the residual stream across iterations, reported as residual
explosion and late-training loss spikes, and attributed to the spectral norm of the injection path
\cite{parcae}. The obvious reading of Wave 1's null is that $K{=}3$ is too shallow for a stabilizer
to have anything to stabilize.

Wave 3 tests that reading by making loop count the independent variable. It takes A1 --- Wave 2's
best loop --- and runs it at $K{=}3$, $K{=}6$ and $K{=}12$ against three refinements implemented for
this wave: \textbf{stabilized recurrence} (the update becomes $h \leftarrow \bar{A} h + \bar{B} e +
R(h)$ with $\bar{A} = \exp(-\Delta \odot \exp(\log A))$, so every transition eigenvalue lies in
$(0,1)$ by construction \cite{parcae}), an \textbf{injection norm} on the group input, and
\textbf{FiLM iteration conditioning} (a learned per-iteration scale and shift, telling the shared
weights which iteration they are executing \cite{depthcond}). Every arm trains on Wave 2's budget,
tokenizer, data and global batch, and is compared against the plain-loop control \emph{at its own
$K$}. The refinements cost under 0.015\% of the model's parameters.

\paragraph{Depth has an optimum, and it is not at the deep end.} The plain loop improves from 2.4916
at $K{=}3$ to 2.4815 at $K{=}6$, then \emph{worsens} to 2.5005 at $K{=}12$ --- non-monotonic, and
the $K{=}12$ point is worse than $K{=}3$ despite executing three times as many layers per token. No
$K{=}12$ arm, refined or not, beats the best $K{=}6$ arm. Whatever looping buys in this architecture,
it is exhausted somewhere near $K{=}6$, and past that more iterations of the same weights actively
cost quality as well as compute. (The $K{=}12$ half of that curve carries the data-order caveat
above; the reversal is 0.0190 nats, several times seed noise, but a controlled version would need
$K{=}12$ on one node, which the 24h limit forbids.)

\paragraph{The instability is real, and appears exactly where predicted.} Gradient norms are flat and
small at $K{=}3$ and $K{=}6$ --- maxima of 0.4 and 5.1, with \emph{zero} logged steps above 100 in
any arm --- and then explode at $K{=}12$, where the plain loop peaks at 85{,}782 and spends 104
logged steps above 100. The onset between $K{=}6$ and $K{=}8$ is precisely where \cite{parcae}
reports it. This also retrospectively explains the $K{=}6$ result: the stabilizer had nothing to
stabilize there, so its 1.8 s.d.\ was never a fair test.

\paragraph{But the stabilizer does not stabilize it.} Given a real instability to fix, the
eigenvalue-bounded recurrence fixes neither the gradients nor the loss. It leaves the loss unchanged
($-0.0003$, 0.2 s.d.) and produces \emph{more} large-gradient steps than the plain loop it replaces
(229 above 100, against 104). What suppresses the excursions is FiLM iteration conditioning, which
carries no stability guarantee at all: it cuts the peak gradient norm 37-fold, from 85{,}782 to
2{,}343, and is the only refinement that improves the loss at every depth
($-0.0001$, $-0.0079$, $-0.0109$, growing with $K$ as predicted). We take this as evidence that the
binding constraint on a deep loop here is not the residual stream's magnitude but the shared
weights' inability to tell iterations apart --- though our gradient norms are a proxy for, not a
measurement of, the residual growth \cite{parcae} actually bounds, and a single seed per arm cannot
separate the two mechanisms cleanly.

\paragraph{The injection norm is harmful, and the harm dominates combinations.} Normalizing the
injected group input --- ablated as a positive in \cite{parcae} --- is the worst intervention tested.
It is the weakest $K{=}6$ arm (0.6 s.d.), and at $K{=}12$ it costs $+0.0475$ nats, 22.6 s.d.\ in the
wrong direction, with the worst gradient behaviour of any arm (563 steps above 100). It also
poisons the full stack: FiLM alone is worth $-0.0109$, but FiLM together with the recurrence and the
norm lands at $+0.0186$, so the norm's harm exceeds FiLM's benefit rather than partially offsetting
it. Refinements here are not additive, and a bundled ``all refinements'' arm would have hidden the
one component doing damage. That is the practical lesson of this wave: the $K{=}3$ pair alone, which
is how the earlier waves were designed, would have concluded ``refinements do nothing'' and stopped.

\paragraph{What this does not establish.} Every Wave 3 arm is a single run, and the s.d.\ column is
borrowed from a different arm's replication. Differences of a few tenths of a standard deviation ---
the recurrence at either depth, the $K{=}3$ pair --- are not results. The three large effects (FiLM
at $K{=}12$ at 5.2 s.d., the injection norm at 22.6, the bundle at 8.9) are far enough outside that
floor to be believed, and the gradient-norm separation spans four orders of magnitude, but the
ordering among the smaller effects should not be read as one. Nothing here has been replicated at a
second seed.

\paragraph{A replication, and a wider noise floor.} The $K{=}3$ control is architecturally identical
to Wave 2's A1 --- same pattern, budget, tokenizer, data, node count and sampler seed, verified by
diffing the two configs down to a naming difference and an inert empty weight-decay group --- so it
is an independent fourth seed of that arm. It lands at 2.4916 against A1's three-seed mean of
2.4951, which is $2.6\times$ the s.d.\ estimated from those three runs. Pooling all four gives a mean
of 2.4942 and an s.d.\ of \textbf{0.0021} against 0.0013 from three; the new point is then an
unremarkable 1.3 s.d.\ from the pooled mean. This is a concrete instance of the caution attached to
Table~\ref{tab:wave2}: a standard deviation from $n=3$ is uncertain by roughly half its value, and
0.0021 is the figure used throughout this section. The Wave 2 conclusions are unaffected --- A1's
lead over A4 remains $2.4\times$ the pooled s.d.


\section{Limitations}

\paragraph{Budget.} Wave 1's 557M tokens was roughly 12\% of a compute-optimal allocation for a
225M-active model; Wave 2's 4.997B closes most of that gap for active parameters ($\sim$20
tokens/parameter $\Rightarrow$ $\sim$4.5--5.6B) but not for total parameters ($\sim$20--40B wanted
at 1.03--1.96B total), so even Wave 2 is not a converged-budget result. The published loop results
this work compares against are reported at 250B tokens and above, still $\sim$50$\times$ beyond
Wave 2.

\paragraph{Seeds.} Table~\ref{tab:main}'s A6a (Wave 1, 4 seeds) varies weight initialization only,
data order held fixed; run-to-run noise under a varying data seed can only be larger, so
Table~\ref{tab:noise} is a lower bound. Wave 2 now has its own partial seed-noise floor
(Section~\ref{sec:wave2-seeds}, Table~\ref{tab:wave2seeds}): A1, A2 and A3 are fully replicated (3
seeds each on held-out loss), with five of the twelve extra runs discarded and re-run after a
scheduling artifact (a cancel-then-warmstart resume, traced to a checkpointing defect on the tied
embedding/output weight -- see Section~\ref{sec:wave2-seeds} for the full account) made them
non-replicates. The measured s.d.\ ranges from 0.0010 to 0.0050 across the six arms; at $n=3$ each
of those estimates is itself uncertain by roughly half its value, so the spread \emph{between} the
arms' s.d.s should not be over-read. Every row of Table~\ref{tab:main} outside A6a, and every anchor
row (N1--N4) and A0 in Table~\ref{tab:wave2}, remains $n=1$ --- which is why the anchor-vs-loop gaps,
the strongest claim in this paper, still rest on one seed on the anchor side. The Wave 1 finding that a nominally
significant reasoning-suite result did not survive four replications (Finding~4) is the strongest
reason not to over-read any of these single-seed numbers, including the ones that still lack a
noise floor.

\paragraph{Confounded budget scaling.} Wave 2 changed tokenizer, dataset composition and hardware
layout alongside the token budget (Section~\ref{sec:wave2}), so it is not a controlled scaling
ladder on Wave 1. The robustness claims in Section~\ref{sec:wave2} are therefore about
\emph{qualitative rankings} (loop beats baseline, anchor beats loop) surviving a bundle of
simultaneous changes, not about a clean budget effect isolated from everything else.

\paragraph{Incomplete arm coverage.} Wave 1's gap here --- the three single-operator loops and their
anchors were never run --- is closed by Wave 2 (A1--A3, N1--N3, all eleven arms now complete), which
is the direct answer to the titular question that Wave 1 could not give. One gap remains: dedicated
anchors for A4 and A5 (the two mixed Mamba-containing loops) still do not exist in either wave, so
those two comparisons still rest on the loop-vs-baseline gap alone rather than a loop-vs-fresh-
weights one.

\paragraph{Reasoning measures.} The synthetic suite is under-powered here, and the $p$-hop task may
additionally be mis-scaled: at a prompt length of 256 over a 26-symbol alphabet the query appears
about 9.8 times, so even a perfectly functioning induction head returns a mixture over roughly ten
candidate successors and lands near 10\% --- which is approximately where the best models sit. A
shorter prompt would make the most-recent match near-unique and give the task room to discriminate.
This applies unchanged to Wave 2, whose $p$-hop-1 accuracies (0.06--0.16) sit in the same band.

\section{Conclusion}

At a matched 557M-token budget, depth-wise weight sharing in a hybrid Mamba--Transformer MoE stack
gives a small but reliable improvement in held-out loss over an unlooped model of the same size,
while remaining behind an iso-FLOP model with fresh weights --- buying memory rather than quality.
The hybrid architecture itself, however, is worth considerably more than the loop: it beats an
iso-FLOP dense transformer by an order of magnitude larger a margin than any difference among the
looped variants, though part of that advantage is returned as lower hardware utilization.

Both of those results reproduce at $\sim$9$\times$ the budget, despite Wave 2 simultaneously
changing tokenizer, data and hardware layout: every looped variant still beats the unlooped
baseline, and a fresh-weight anchor still beats its iso-execution loop, now confirmed for all four
matched pairs Wave 2 has, including a direct re-confirmation of Wave 1's original attention+MoE
pair. What is new at the larger budget is the answer to the question the title asks. Completing the
three single-operator loops that Wave 1 lacked shows the effect concentrates in one operator:
looping Mamba alone is the best loop of any kind tried, mixed loops that include Mamba come next,
and looping attention alone is the worst, barely ahead of no loop at all. Depth in this architecture
is worth spending on the recurrent operator, not the quadratic one --- and the same operator shows
the largest gap between what a loop buys and what real fresh depth buys, suggesting both findings
trace back to the same property of the Mamba operator rather than being two unrelated results.
Twelve further runs (two extra seeds of A1--A6 each) were launched to check this ranking against a
measured seed-noise floor rather than $n=1$. All six looped arms are now replicated at three seeds
(s.d.\ 0.0010--0.0050 on held-out loss), and the ranking they produce is identical to the
single-seed one. A1's lead and A3's deficit are several times the pooled noise, so the titular claim
holds; the interior ordering is within two pooled standard deviations and is not resolved. The
Wave-1-vs-Wave-2 A4/A5 swap is confirmed on the Wave 2 side at $t = 4.9$ on 2.2 d.f., but its cause
is not identified: four things changed between the waves at once.

Wave 3 (Section~\ref{sec:wave3}) returns to the one negative result Wave 1 left hanging --- that
loop refinements do nothing --- and shows it was a statement about loop depth rather than about
refinements. Pushed to $K{=}12$, three things separate that the shallower waves could not. Loop depth
turns out to have an optimum near $K{=}6$, past which more iterations of the same weights cost
quality as well as compute. The instability the literature predicts is real and switches on between
$K{=}6$ and $K{=}12$, exactly where reported. And the refinement with the theory behind it does not
fix it: the eigenvalue-bounded recurrence leaves both loss and gradients where it found them, while
FiLM iteration conditioning --- which guarantees nothing --- cuts the peak gradient norm 37-fold and
is the only refinement that helps at every depth. One component, the injection norm, is actively
harmful and dominates every combination containing it, which is the strongest argument in this paper
for ablating refinements individually rather than as a bundle: the bundled arm looks like a modest
failure, and hides a large benefit cancelled by a larger harm.

The methodological finding may be the more useful one, and this wave supplied its sharpest example
yet. Held-out loss and the reasoning suite differ in measurement resolution by more than two orders
of magnitude relative to the effects of interest, and a nominally 3.9-sigma result computed from
sampling error alone did not survive four replications. Wave 2 adds that a headline ranking can also
shift at the margins under a budget change bundled with other changes --- A4 and A5 swap places
between waves, by 0.005 nats at 557M and 0.014 at 5B, well inside what a change of tokenizer or data
plausibly moves. Then the seed-replication run meant to check exactly that nearly introduced a worse
problem than the one it was measuring: five runs cancelled mid-training and resumed with
\texttt{modalities warmstart} came back 0.04--0.055 nats better than their own first seeds --- enough,
had it been averaged in unexamined, to make a mixed loop look better than the pure-Mamba loop and
overturn this paper's own headline result, for a reason with nothing to do with architecture. It was
caught only by checking that the numbers a repeated-seed experiment produced looked like noise, and
finding that five of them did not. The cause took three wrong explanations to pin down --- it is
neither extra gradient steps nor a reset learning-rate schedule, both of which were checked and
found identical, but a defect in the checkpoint round-trip for the tied embedding and output weight
--- which is itself the point: an anomaly can be established as real long before it is understood,
and excluding the affected runs did not have to wait on the diagnosis.

Wave 3 then closes the loop on Wave 1's negative result, and supplies two more cautions. Making loop
count the independent variable confirms the interaction that was predicted --- refinements do
nothing at $K{=}3$ and something measurable at $K{=}6$ --- while refuting the mechanism that was
predicted alongside it: the refinement with the theory behind it, a recurrence whose eigenvalues are
bounded by construction, is not the one that helps, and the gradient norms show the plain loop was
never unstable at this depth for it to rescue. The largest effect in that wave is neither refinement
nor architecture but simply looping twice as often, which is worth more than every refinement
combined and costs 1.68$\times$ the compute to get. And Wave 3's control, being an exact replicate of
Wave 2's best arm, raised that arm's measured seed s.d.\ by half again merely by existing. Any claim
from a suite of this kind should be accompanied by a measured seed-noise floor before it is believed
--- including, and especially, one's own --- the noise floor should be checked for artifacts before
it is trusted either, and a floor estimated from three runs should be treated as the rough figure it
is.
